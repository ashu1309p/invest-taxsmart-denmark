The positivliste: the list that sets your fund's tax

SKAT keeps an official list of equity-based funds (the ABIS list). Whether your fund is on it or off it decides which tax it gets, and the list is refreshed every year.

✓ ON the list
aktieindkomst [share income], 27% / 42%. Allowed inside an ASK. Usually the win for an adult.
OFF the list
kapitalindkomst [capital income], ~37-42%, taxed yearly (lager). Not allowed in an ASK. Usually the win for a child's frikort.

Paste the exact ISIN. Each share class (ACC, DIST, hedged) has its own.

How and when it changes. SKAT publishes the list around 15 December for the coming year, and can amend it mid-year. Funds get added and removed, which flips a fund's tax from one January to the next.

See a worked example: a fund going off, then on

Illustration only. These are real funds, used to show the mechanism, not a claim that they changed status. Always verify the current list yourself.

Case 1, a fund drops OFF the list. Say the iShares Core MSCI World ETF (Acc), ISIN IE00B4L5Y983, is on the list this year and SKAT drops it next December. From 1 January, in a normal depot its yearly gain switches from aktieindkomst (27/42%) to kapitalindkomst (~37-42%), and it can no longer sit in an ASK. Adjust: an adult holding it inside an ASK would typically sell it there and buy a still-listed world ETF instead (an internal ASK trade does not use up your deposit ceiling); an adult in a normal depot might move future buys to a still-listed equivalent, or accept the higher rate. For a child's frikort depot the drop is often fine, even better, because kapitalindkomst is exactly what the personfradrag absorbs.
Case 2, a fund is ADDED to the list. Now the reverse: an off-list accumulating world ETF that a child holds for the frikort gets added next December. From January its gain becomes aktieindkomst (27/42%) with no personfradrag relief, so the child pays 27% from the first krone and the frikort trick stops working for it. Adjust: point the child's new contributions at a fund that is still off the list. For an adult the same change is good news, the fund is now cheaper and can go inside an ASK.

Your yearly habit. Every December or January, and before any new purchase, look up the exact ISIN of each fund you hold or plan to buy on skat.dk. If a holding has changed status, note its new tax and ask an advokat/revisor before any sizeable move.

⚠ Check the exact ISIN: every share class (ACC, DIST, hedged, unhedged) has its OWN ISIN, and the positivliste works per ISIN. The unhedged class being on the list says nothing about the hedged class. Verify your exact ISIN before buying.

Reading a fund's name: ACC vs DIST, hedged vs unhedged

Every ETF name carries these switches. In Denmark one of them changes the tax story, the other mostly doesn't.

ACC [accumulating]Dividends stay inside the fund and compound automatically.
Nothing is paid out, so there is no dividend to tax separately: everything lands in the yearly value gain (lager). For children this is the safe construction: no payout means nothing for the parent-attribution rule to catch.
DIST [distributing, udloddende]Dividends are paid out in cash, typically 1-4 times a year.
For adults the dividend is just part of the same income type, but you must reinvest it yourself. For children, dividends on parent-gifted money are taxed at the PARENT until age 18 (kildeskatteloven § 5, stk. 2). Special case: Danish distributing equity funds (IMB) are realisation-taxed aktieindkomst [share income], a different regime from ETFs.
UnhedgedYou hold the fund's currencies as they are; exchange rates move your return.
For long equity horizons many investors accept currency swings: they tend to even out over decades, and DKK is pegged to EUR, so EUR exposure behaves almost like DKK. Hedging or not has no effect on the Danish tax regime.
Hedged [valutaafdækket]Built-in currency insurance smooths exchange-rate swings, for a small ongoing cost.
Mostly relevant for bond funds, where currency noise can swamp the modest return. Hedging costs roughly 0.1-0.3% per year on top of the ÅOP. It does NOT change the tax regime, but see the ISIN warning below.
📈 Worked example: ACC vs DIST on the same fund

A world fund returns about 7% in a year: roughly 5% price growth and 2% dividends. Say you hold 100,000 kr.

ACC (accumulating)Nothing is paid out. The full ~7,000 kr (5,000 growth + 2,000 dividends) stays in the fund and compounds. A child is taxed on the yearly gain as the child, so the frikort can absorb it; nothing to reinvest, nothing to self-declare.
DIST (distributing)About 2,000 kr is paid to you as cash. For an adult it is the same tax, but you must reinvest the 2,000 kr yourself or it sits idle. For a child whose money was gifted by a parent, that 2,000 kr is taxed at the PARENT until age 18 (kildeskatteloven § 5, stk. 2), and the bank does not report it, so you must self-declare.

For a child, accumulating avoids a parent tax bill and the paperwork; for an adult it saves the manual reinvesting. That is why children's accounts use accumulating funds only.

📈 Worked example: shares flat, the dollar moves 5%

You put 10,000 kr in a world fund and over the year the shares themselves go nowhere (0%). Only the dollar moves against the krone.

If the dollar rises 5%
Unhedged+5%, ends at 10,500 kr (you ride the currency up)
Hedgedabout 0% minus the hedge fee, 9,980 kr
If the dollar falls 5%
Unhedged-5%, ends at 9,500 kr (you ride the currency down)
Hedgedabout 0% minus the hedge fee, 9,980 kr

Hedged hands you the same result either way: it removes the swing, up and down, for a small certain cost (~0.1-0.3% a year, about 0.2% here). Over a long stock horizon these moves tend to even out, so many investors skip hedging on shares. On bonds, where the return is small, currency noise can swamp it, so hedging is more common there.

⚠ Child trap: a distributing fund bought with parent-gifted money leaks parent-taxed dividends, even small ones, and the bank does not report this for you. Children's accounts: accumulating only.
