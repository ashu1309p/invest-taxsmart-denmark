The positivliste: the list that sets your fund's tax

Two almost identical world ETFs can be taxed in completely different ways in Denmark. The deciding factor is a single official list.

Tax year 2026 Every rate and amount below is for this tax year.

What the positivliste actually is

SKAT keeps an official list of equity-based investment funds, formally the ABIS list (aktiebaserede investeringsselskaber). In everyday language people call it the positivliste, the "positive list". Whether a given fund is on it or off it decides which kind of income its gains are taxed as, and the list is refreshed every year.

That single yes-or-no fact ripples through everything else: the tax rate you pay, whether you can hold the fund inside an Aktiesparekonto (ASK), and whether it is a smart pick for an adult or for a child. None of it is about the fund being "good" or "bad" as an investment; it is purely about how Denmark labels it for tax.

On the list vs off the list: the tax flips

Here is the core of it. The same gain is taxed under one of two regimes, depending on the list:

On the list -> aktieindkomst (share income) Gains are taxed as share income at 27% up to 79,400 kr per person (158,800 kr for a married couple) and 42% above that. On-list funds are allowed inside an ASK. For most adults this is the cheaper outcome.
Off the list -> kapitalindkomst (capital income) Gains are taxed as capital income at roughly 37-42%, calculated yearly on the change in value (lager). Off-list funds cannot sit inside an ASK. For a child using the frikort, this is often the better outcome.

So an on-list and an off-list fund holding nearly the same shares can land in different income types with different rates. That is why checking the list is worth the two minutes it takes.

What it means for an adult vs a child

For an adult, on-list share income is usually the goal. The 27% rate is low, the ASK wrapper (which only accepts on-list funds) is taxed even more gently at a flat 17% a year, and the step up to 42% only begins above 79,400 kr of share income in a year.

For a child the logic often flips. A child has a personal allowance (personfradrag) of 54,100 kr for 2026, and it is capital income that this allowance, used as a frikort, soaks up. An off-list fund's kapitalindkomst can therefore be taxed at very little, or nothing, inside the allowance. An on-list fund taxed as aktieindkomst gets no personfradrag relief, so the child pays 27% from the very first krone and the frikort trick stops working for it.

It is per-ISIN, not per-fund

The list works per ISIN, the unique identifier of one specific share class, not per fund family. A single fund can have several share classes: accumulating (ACC) and distributing (DIST), currency-hedged and unhedged, and each has its own ISIN. The unhedged class being on the list tells you nothing about the hedged class.

Always check the exact ISIN of the share class you actually intend to buy, before you buy. You can paste it straight into the ISIN checker on the Plan page.

How and when the list changes

SKAT publishes the list around 15 December for the coming year, and can amend it during the year. Funds are added and removed, which flips a fund's tax from one January to the next. A fund you bought as on-list share income can become off-list capital income the next year, or the other way around, without you doing anything.

Two quick scenarios

Illustration only. These describe the mechanism, not a claim that any specific fund changed status. Always verify the current list yourself.

A fund drops off the list

Suppose a world ETF you hold is on the list this year and SKAT drops it next December. From 1 January, in a normal depot, its yearly gain switches from share income to capital income, and it can no longer sit in an ASK. An adult holding it inside an ASK would typically sell it there and buy a still-listed world ETF instead (an internal ASK trade does not use up your deposit ceiling). For a child's frikort depot the drop is often fine, even better, because capital income is exactly what the allowance absorbs.

A fund is added to the list

Now the reverse: an off-list accumulating world ETF that a child holds for the frikort gets added next December. From January its gain becomes share income with no personfradrag relief, so the child pays 27% from the first krone. The fix is to point the child's new contributions at a fund that is still off the list. For an adult the same change is good news: the fund is now cheaper and can go inside an ASK.

Key takeaways

  • The positivliste (ABIS list) decides whether a fund's gains are share income (27/42%) or capital income (~37-42%).
  • On-list is usually best for an adult and can go in an ASK; off-list is often best for a child's frikort and cannot.
  • It works per ISIN, so every share class differs - check the exact one.
  • The list changes around 15 December and can change mid-year, so re-check before every purchase.
